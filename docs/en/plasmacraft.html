PlasmaCraft, recipe-authoring software

The context

A medical device manufacturer was developing a cold-plasma sterilization machine driven by a PLC. Its teams had to program cycle recipes sequence by sequence, with no room for error.

Four key features

A

Error-proof input

  • Recipes of up to 100 sequences, with four parameters controlled over time: RF power, two gas flows and the magnetic field.
  • Checks against the machine's limits and step size (0 to 3,600 s per sequence, 0 to 500 W in 1 W steps, 0 to 5 sccm and 0 to 22 G in 0.1 steps), with a precise message on the faulty field.
  • A unique ID, name, author and date for each recipe.
B

Ramps and chart

  • “Progressive” option: a setpoint change split into 5 to 25 steps that add up exactly; refused when there are more steps than seconds or than units of change.
  • A dynamic curve using linear interpolation, sampled every 1 s under 1,000 s of recipe, then every 2 s, 3 s and finally 5 s beyond 6,000 s to stay smooth.
C

Batch editing

  • Selection through auto-generated checkboxes: delete, swap (durations trade places, cumulative times stay right), duplicate as a block (indexed copies, refused beyond 100 sequences), edit in place with the same checks.
  • A unique index per sequence, to move rows around without breaking the formatting.
D

Secure transfer

  • TXT export for the PLC, and import of XLSM recipes created on another workstation.
  • Recipes saved as XLSM files locked with a password, unique name checked against the database, automatic PDF capture.

Demo · algorithm rewritten in JavaScript

The step problem

The PLC can only do “all or nothing”. To simulate a ramp, the change has to be split into steps at the machine's resolution (1 W for power, 0.1 for gas flow and field), and the steps have to add up exactly. Dividing then rounding is not enough: that is the bug I had to fix. This demo was rewritten in JavaScript from the principle of the algorithm.

An example from the internship report: with the first version, 19.2 G in 5 steps gave 3.7 + 3.9 + 3.9 + 3.9 + 3.9 = 19.3. The exact split gives 3.8 + 3.8 + 3.8 + 3.9 + 3.9 = 19.2. In the delivered version, parameters with a 0.1 step rely on a precomputed table that gives, for every change and every number of steps, increments that add up exactly.
